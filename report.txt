Part A — Open-access PDFs on “AI text detection” (high-value starting set)
Below is a curated set of downloadable PDF publications that show the main families of AI text detection methods (statistical, neural classifiers, watermarking, benchmarking, and reliability testing). These are widely cited and useful as a “core reading list.”
1) Surveys (good for method taxonomy)


“A Survey on LLM‑Generated Text Detection: Necessity, Methods, and Future Directions” (PDF) — broad taxonomy including watermarking, statistics-based detectors, neural detectors, and human-assisted methods; discusses OOD and adversarial issues. [aclanthology.org], [arxiv.org]

PDF: ACL Anthology PDF



“Detecting AI‑Generated Text: Factors Influencing Detectability with Current Methods” (PDF) — synthesizes factors affecting detectability and practical recommendations; covers watermarking, statistical/stylistic, ML classification and dataset issues. [aclanthology.org], [arxiv.org]

PDF: arXiv abstract (PDF link inside)



2) Benchmarks & evaluation frameworks (where detectors succeed/fail)


RAID: “A Shared Benchmark for Robust Evaluation of Machine‑Generated Text Detectors” (PDF) — massive benchmark (millions of generations) evaluating open + closed detectors; shows robustness failures under changes in decoding, penalties, and adversarial transformations; includes commercial detectors in evaluation set. [aclanthology.org], [arxiv.org]

PDF: ACL Anthology PDF



“Testing of Detection Tools for AI‑Generated Text” (PDF) — large comparative test across many tools including commercial systems; concludes tools are not reliably accurate and are sensitive to obfuscation/translation effects. [arxiv.org], [aclanthology.org]

PDF: arXiv abstract (PDF link inside)



“How Reliable Are AI‑Generated‑Text Detectors? … Using Evasive Soft Prompts” (PDF) — a reliability assessment approach that demonstrates detectors can be misled; useful for understanding threat models and robustness testing (again, I’m not turning this into bypass instructions). [raid-bench.xyz], [aclanthology.org]

PDF: ACL Anthology PDF



3) Canonical “statistical detection” / interpretability

GLTR: “Statistical Detection and Visualization of Generated Text” (PDF) — classic baseline: uses token rank / likelihood patterns (white-box-ish with a reference LM) to help humans detect generated text; shows human-subject improvements. [aclanthology.org], [arxiv.org]

PDF: arXiv PDF or ACL entry (PDF link)



4) Watermarking (a different detection strategy: detect the “mark,” not the style)


SynthID‑Text: “Scalable watermarking for identifying large language model outputs” (PDF, Nature) — production-oriented watermarking that modifies sampling, with efficient detection that doesn’t require the generator model at detection time. [arxiv.org], [arxiv.org]

PDF: Nature PDF



“Watermarking Large Language Models: An Unbiased and Low‑risk Method” (PDF) — proposes STA‑1 watermarking with statistical guarantees; discusses robustness and detection efficiency. [aclanthology.org], [arxiv.org]

PDF: ACL Anthology PDF



“Adaptive Text Watermark for Large Language Models” (PDF via PMLR) — adaptive watermarking that targets high-entropy positions for quality/robustness trade-offs. [aclanthology.org], [arxiv.org]

PDF: PMLR page (PDF link)

What Turnitin publicly reveals about how it detects AI text
From Turnitin’s own documentation and associated public PDFs, the following elements are explicitly described:

Modeling approach


Turnitin characterizes its detector as a deep learning / transformer-based system and discusses concepts like perplexity, burstiness, recall, and false positive rate in its technical whitepaper. [arxiv.org], [arxiv.org]
Turnitin documentation also indicates that language-specific models exist (e.g., Spanish and Japanese detectors trained for those languages and to detect outputs from specific LLM versions). [guides.turnitin.com], [guides.ith...ticate.com]


Document processing & scoring granularity


A public PDF guide describing Turnitin’s AI detection workflow states that submissions are broken into overlapping text segments and that Turnitin assigns sentence-level scores (0–1) which are aggregated into an overall prediction. [aclanthology.org], [nature.com]
Turnitin’s own product guides emphasize that AI scores should not be used as the sole basis for adverse actions and highlight known issues around false positives and how they are mitigated in reporting. [guides.turnitin.com], [nature.com]


Thresholding & false positive mitigation


Turnitin release notes and iThenticate/Turnitin guides describe a policy choice: scores below 20% are no longer surfaced (shown with an asterisk) to reduce misinterpretation where false positives are more likely. [guides.turnitin.com], [guides.ith...ticate.com]
The same documentation describes file requirements (e.g., minimum prose word count) because short texts are less reliable for detection. [guides.ith...ticate.com], [guides.turnitin.com]


Detection categories beyond “AI-written”


Turnitin/iThenticate documents describe a two-part breakdown:

“AI-generated only” (and may include text modified by an “AI bypasser/humanizer”)
“AI-generated text that was AI-paraphrased” (e.g., paraphrasing/word-spinners) [guides.ith...ticate.com], [guides.turnitin.com]


Turnitin release notes explicitly mention updates that add AI paraphrasing detection and later AI bypasser tool detection to reporting categories. [guides.turnitin.com], [guides.ith...ticate.com]

iThenticate-specific disclosures (Turnitin’s research integrity product)
iThenticate’s own user guides (public web documentation) confirm that it uses Turnitin’s AI writing detection capability inside the enhanced Similarity Report experience and repeats the same caveats on misidentification risk and the need for human judgment. 
Additionally, a publicly available iThenticate 2.0 slide deck (PDF) discusses inclusion of AI writing and AI paraphrasing detection in the workflow. [guides.ith...ticate.com], [aclanthology.org] [aclanthology.org], [proceeding....mlr.press]
Bottom line for Turnitin/iThenticate (from public sources):
Turnitin discloses high-level architecture themes (transformer/deep learning), reporting mechanics (segmenting, highlighting), risk controls (thresholding under 20%), and feature scope (AI paraphrase + bypasser detection), but does not fully disclose training datasets, exact feature sets, or model weights—i.e., the implementation remains largely proprietary beyond what is in the whitepaper and guides. [arxiv.org], [guides.turnitin.com], [guides.ith...ticate.com]

2) GPTZero — employee publications + method disclosures
Employee-authored research publication (downloadable PDF)

“GPTZero: Robust Detection of LLM‑Generated Texts” (arXiv, PDF) — authored by a team including Edward Tian (GPTZero) and describes GPTZero as an “industrial AI detection solution,” highlighting a hierarchical, multi-task architecture and robustness work (multi-tier automated red teaming). [arxiv.org], [proceeding....mlr.press]

PDF: arXiv abstract (PDF link inside)



GPTZero’s public explanation of method signals (company disclosure)

GPTZero publicly explains that early versions relied on a statistical approach and that perplexity + burstiness form a “first layer” of detection, alongside other indicators in its upgraded system. [turnitin.co.uk], [arxiv.org]
GPTZero’s explanation defines perplexity as a measure of how likely a reference model would choose the observed tokens and burstiness as variation in those patterns across a document, positioning them as computationally efficient signals. [turnitin.co.uk], [bing.com]

Bottom line for GPTZero (from public sources):
GPTZero discloses both (a) a research paper claiming a hierarchical multi-task detector and robustness via red teaming, and (b) a public-facing description of layered detection including perplexity/burstiness plus additional components (e.g., deep learning + search). [arxiv.org], [turnitin.co.uk]

Part C — What these disclosures imply about “methods used” (defensive synthesis)
1) Vendor methods map onto the academic taxonomy
The major academic survey(s) categorize detection into: statistics-based detectors, neural classifiers, watermarking, and human-assisted approaches. 
Public vendor disclosures align with this: [aclanthology.org], [arxiv.org]

Turnitin emphasizes deep learning/transformer-based detection with reporting controls and additional classifiers for paraphrase/bypass patterns. [arxiv.org], [guides.turnitin.com]
GPTZero publicly acknowledges statistical signals (perplexity/burstiness) and claims multi-task neural architecture in its arXiv publication. [turnitin.co.uk], [arxiv.org]

2) Robustness remains the biggest practical challenge
Independent benchmarking work (RAID) shows detectors can be “high performing” under default conditions yet fail under:

domain shift,
model shift,
decoding strategy changes,
paraphrasing/adversarial transformations. 
Similarly, large comparative testing finds tools are often not reliable enough to be treated as definitive proof and are impacted by obfuscation/translation. [aclanthology.org], [arxiv.org] [arxiv.org], [aclanthology.org]

3) Vendor “risk controls” are as important as the model
Turnitin/iThenticate’s public guides reveal operational mitigations: minimum-length requirements, supported “qualifying prose” definitions, and suppressing low-percentage scores to reduce false-positive misinterpretation. 
This is consistent with benchmark findings that false positive rates and calibration thresholds materially affect perceived “accuracy.” [guides.ith...ticate.com], [guides.turnitin.com] [aclanthology.org], [arxiv.org]
